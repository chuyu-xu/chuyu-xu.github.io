01 Key findings

Two outcomes. Different patterns.

The paper compares employment and wage outcomes between women with and without resident children. Its country-specific employment models report a larger negative association in Canada.

Table 2 reports these coefficients. The Canadian Heckman estimate is not statistically significant as reported. The U.S. significance reporting conflicts across sources; see the source checks below.

Interpretation matters. The supplied code uses different sample restrictions and weights across these models. Their differences cannot be attributed solely to the selection correction.

A Canadian extension also reports a positive association with the voluntary-exit category. This is an exploratory result: the category definitions and reference group need verification before interpreting it as a reason for the employment gap.

04 Implications

A single metric does not tell the whole story

Employment and wages capture different dimensions of labour-market outcomes. Looking at both helps distinguish participation patterns from the earnings of the people observed working.

The broader analytical lesson is to check definitions, sample coverage and model assumptions before comparing groups. That discipline is transferable to audience, consumer and business insights. Family-policy differences provide context here, but their causal contribution remains a hypothesis.

05 Limits & disclosure

What the evidence can—and cannot—say

This is an observational cross-country comparison, not a before-and-after study of the same women. Resident-child indicators are imperfect proxies for motherhood. Country interactions cannot isolate a policy effect, and the Heckman exclusion restriction requires a substantive assumption.

Source checks that affect interpretation

  • Sample and weights — confirmed in the printed code. Logit and OLS commands specify female restrictions and survey weights; the Heckman commands do not. Reported U.S. OLS and Heckman observation counts are 390,052 and 1,289,778, covering different outcome/selection samples. Their exact composition cannot be established without the original data and logs.
  • Significance — unresolved reporting conflict. For the U.S. Heckman coefficient, Table 2 marks two stars while the prose says p < 0.01. Its rounded coefficient and standard error (−0.0084 / 0.0028) imply |z| ≈ 3 and a normal-approximation p ≈ 0.0027. This does not reconcile the original output; no definitive p-value is asserted here.
  • Definitions and execution — further verification needed. The prose gives conflicting age ranges. Wage units, employment coding and exit-category mapping need codebook checks; the printout also repeats an existing variable definition. The data files exceeded this environment’s transfer limit, so models have not been rerun.
